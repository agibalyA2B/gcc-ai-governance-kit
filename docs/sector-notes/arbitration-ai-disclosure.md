# Sector note: AI use and disclosure in arbitration

**Who this is for:** teams whose AI tools prepare claims, submissions, expert reports or evidence that may end up in
arbitration, adjudication or a dispute board. This is common in construction and commercial disputes in the GCC.
The guideline is written for arbitration; for adjudication and dispute boards, apply it by analogy and check the
rules that govern the process.

**Source:** Chartered Institute of Arbitrators (Ciarb), *Guideline on the Use of AI in Arbitration (2025)*, updated
September 2025: <https://www.ciarb.org/media/bpndtcgu/guideline-on-the-use-of-ai-in-arbitration_updated-sept-2025.pdf>.
This note paraphrases it and cites articles by number only; read the guideline itself for its wording. It is a
guideline, not law. It applies to a case where the parties or the tribunal adopt it, and its appendices include
model agreements and procedural orders for that purpose.

## What the guideline says, in short
- **Disclosure may be required** where using an AI tool could affect the evidence or the outcome, or would hand
  over a duty owed to the tribunal or another party. The aim is transparency, and ultimately the integrity of the
  process and the enforceability of the award (Art. 7.1–7.2).
- **Tribunals can set the rules.** After consulting the parties, a tribunal may direct which AI use must be
  disclosed, when, to whom and by when. This can cover party-appointed experts and fact witnesses (Art. 7.3). Unless
  ordered otherwise, the duty lasts for the whole proceedings (Art. 7.4). Tribunals may regulate the parties' use of
  AI and record decisions in a procedural order (Art. 4.3, 4.6), and may rule on the use and admissibility of
  AI-generated material (Art. 6).
- **Private use and privilege.** Tribunals may not regulate a party's private use of AI where it does not interfere
  with the proceedings and would be allowed in the local courts (Art. 4.5). When setting or assessing a disclosure
  duty, a tribunal may weigh it against confidentiality and any right to withhold information (Art. 7.5).
- **Responsibility stays with people.** Using an AI tool does not reduce any participant's responsibility or
  accountability, unless the tribunal and the parties expressly agree otherwise in writing (Art. 3.4).
- **Admissibility turns on traceability.** In deciding whether AI-assisted or AI-generated content may enter the
  record, a tribunal may look at whether its source data is on the record, and may ask a party to explain how inputs
  from the record link to the output (Art. 6.6).
- **Non-disclosure has consequences.** If a party does not disclose AI use against a direction, the tribunal may
  inquire, take remedial measures, draw adverse inferences or reflect it in costs (Art. 7.6–7.7).
- **Confidentiality.** Putting confidential case material into a third-party AI tool raises serious confidentiality
  risks (Art. 2.2).
- **Arbitrators' own use.** Arbitrators should not hand their decision-making to AI, remain responsible for the
  award, and are encouraged to consult the parties before using AI tools (Art. 8.2, 8.4, 9.1).

## What to record, per matter
1. Whether the arbitration agreement, the institutional rules or a procedural order address AI use.
2. Which AI tools prepared which submissions, reports or exhibits, and the human who reviewed each one. The
   guideline's model long-form procedural order goes further for high-risk uses: it names the tool, the document,
   the specific passages drafted by generative AI, and certifies that no confidential information was disclosed
   (Appendix B).
3. The disclosure decision and who made it: counsel, not the tool.
4. Where the matter data was processed, and under which confidentiality terms.
5. The link from every AI-assisted assertion back to evidence on the record, so it can be explained if challenged
   (Art. 6.6).

## How it maps to the kit
- Answer **Is it used in a regulated domain?** with *Yes* when a tribunal or regulator governs the outcome. This also
  caps the recommended autonomy.
- Treat counterparty letters and submissions as untrusted input: see CTL-SEC-02 and CTL-SEC-09 (prompt injection,
  red-team testing).
- Use the control-evidence map to hold points 1–5 above as evidence, and keep a person accountable for every position
  the tool drafts.
